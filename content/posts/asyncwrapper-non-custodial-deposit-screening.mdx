[Part 1](/writing/asyncwrapper-anonymous-on-ramp) ended on a question it didn't
answer. If the on-ramp is anonymous, with encrypted recipients matched against
a crowd of decoys, how can this add compliance?

Because of the step in the middle. Splitting the wrap into *record* and
*match-and-mint* opened a gap between "money arrived" and "balance credited",
and that gap is where a deposit gets screened before it becomes a confidential
balance. An atomic `wrap()` has no such gap: transfer and mint are one
transaction, and there is no point in between where a check could run. The same
deferral that made the on-ramp private is what makes it screenable.

## How privacy and compliance can co-exist

Privacy and compliance are usually presented as opposites. Mixers on one side,
where they publish nothing and can refuse nobody, so the pool inherits the
reputation of its worst depositors, and that is what gets them sanctioned.
Fully public data on the other, auditable by anyone including your company's
competitors, or the criminals willing to even kidnap you.

But one design exists that can deliver privacy and compliance in one go. It is
a pool that grants privacy to funds only after screening them. This is the
concept of **Privacy Pools**: prove your funds belong to a vetted set without
revealing which deposit is yours.

AsyncWrapper brings the same equilibrium to confidential ERC-7984 tokens, with
FHE (instead of ZK) and a programmable entry in place of association-set
proofs. The rest of this post is the machinery of that separation.

## Preventing permissionless privacy

OpenZeppelin's ERC-7984 wrapper is permissionless: anyone can call `wrap()` and
mint themselves a confidential balance. Our async wrapper does not have such
functionality.

**A confidential balance can only be created through an entry function gated by
the operator.** Entry stops being a public function and becomes whatever policy
the deployment enforces. The async wrapper contract ships no policy of its own;
leaving the policy design entirely in the hands of the operator.

## Screening happens while the money waits

In our deployment the entry function is restricted to an operator role. A
depositor sends their funds to a special deposit address (more on it below) for
the operator to run its checks: KYC, sanctions screening, source of funds,
whatever the policy requires.

The payer never sends funds to the operator and never calls the contract. They
send their funds to a **counterfactual address**: a CREATE2 address that is
fully determined before any code exists at it. Two inputs go into deriving it,
and both matter later:

```solidity
// who the money is for (encrypted), and a commitment to a secret they hold
commitment     = keccak256(secret, recipient);
depositAddress = create2(keccak256(encryptedRecipient, commitment));
```

Anyone can compute the address. Nobody can move funds out of it until the
wrapper deploys code there. The operator watches for the funding transfer,
screens it, and only if it passes deploys the address and pulls the deposit in:

```solidity
function initWrap(bytes32 encryptedRecipient, bytes32 commitment, uint256 amount)
    external
    onlyOperator
{
    address deposit = deployDepositor(encryptedRecipient, commitment);
    Depositor(deposit).sweepTo(address(this), amount);  // take the funds
    recordNote(deposit, amount, encryptedRecipient);    // still nothing minted
}
```

Funds can sit at that address for as long as screening takes. They are
committed to a recipient but still outside the pool. An atomic wrap has no
equivalent of this window. Its compliance check would have to run synchronously
on-chain, inside the mint, or not at all. Here the decision is made off-chain,
with all the context an off-chain screen has and a contract never will.

## How the deposit addresses are non-custodial

If the operator could keep those funds stuck, this would become outright
custody: declining a deposit would mean seizing it. For it to be non-custodial
means the intended recipient can always get the money, whether the operator
says no, goes offline, or never acts.

The address construction is what guarantees that. Anyone who can reproduce its
two inputs can reclaim the funds in one transaction, with no operator involved:

```solidity
function withdraw(bytes32 secret, address recipient) external {
    // nothing was stored on-chain, so this works even if initWrap never ran
    address deposit = deployDepositor(secret, recipient);

    // pays out to `recipient`, never to whoever sent this transaction
    Depositor(deposit).sweepTo(recipient, token.balanceOf(deposit));
}
```

**The operator decides what enters the pool but cannot confiscate what stays
out.** To a regulator, that is the difference between a screener and a
custodian.

Recovery is callable by anyone and always pays the recipient, so a relayer can
run it for a user who has no gas. A declined deposit cannot be stranded.

## Who holds the secret

The contract doesn't care where the secret comes from, and deployments can
differ here. In [Raycash](/work/raycash), our backend generates it and emails it
to the recipient along with the address to fund. Both sides hold it: support can
help a user who lost the email, and every deposit leaves an off-chain record
with both parties. A deployment that wants the opposite tradeoff lets the
recipient generate the secret and never transmit it; then nobody else can
trigger recovery, and nobody can help when it is lost.

Either way, **the secret is not a key to the money**. The recipient's address is
hashed into the commitment, and the commitment into the deposit address.
Recompute with a different recipient and you don't redirect the deposit, you
derive a different address that holds nothing. The only destination the funds
can ever reach was fixed before they were sent. The worst another secret-holder
can do is trigger the recovery early, paying gas to send the recipient their own
money.

## What's public, what's auditable

To an observer: some addresses received cleartext USDC, and some confidential
mints later happened across decoy batches. The machinery described in
[part 1](/writing/asyncwrapper-anonymous-on-ramp) severs everything between the
two: recipients are encrypted, minted amounts are encrypted and can be zero, and
a spent deposit is indistinguishable from an untouched one.

To the operator: who was screened, what the checks returned, and which deposits
were admitted or declined, with the off-chain records to back it.

|  | Mixer | Transparent wrapper | AsyncWrapper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Observers can trace deposits to recipients | no | yes | no |
| Can refuse a deposit at entry | no | no | yes |
| Protocol takes custody | no | no | no |
| Screening can strand funds | n/a | n/a | no, funds always recoverable |

A mixer fails compliance and a transparent wrapper fails privacy for the same
reason: neither has an entry you can put a policy behind.

## This is a v1

The operator gate is only the simplest policy that works. The primitive
underneath records a note and doesn't care what stands in front of it: an
association-set proof, a multi-party screen, or an attestation quorum would slot
in the same way. And this post covers entry only. What a deployment enforces
after entry, who can hold and receive inside the pool, is a topic for another
post.

None of those choices touch the core claim: a two-step wrap creates a screening
window an atomic wrap cannot have, and unconditional recovery keeps that
screening an admission decision rather than a seizure power.

Encrypting balances was the solved part. The hard part was an entrance that
observers can't trace and a regulated business can still defend: screened,
recorded, impossible to trap funds in. We now have all of them.
