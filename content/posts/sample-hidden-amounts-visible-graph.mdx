A thing I got wrong for longer than I'd like to admit: I thought hiding the
amount was the job.

Say you deposit 5,000 USDC into a confidential token and receive an encrypted
balance. The amount is now a ciphertext. Anyone watching still saw a public
ERC-20 transfer of exactly 5,000 USDC land at a wrapper contract, followed
moments later by one new confidential balance appearing. Nothing decrypted
anything, and everyone knows what you have.

This is the deposit graph, and it survives encryption because it isn't made of
amounts. It's made of timing, ordering, and the fact that one thing followed
another.

The countermeasures are all variations on breaking that correspondence. Batch
deposits so several arrive together. Delay finalisation so the wrap doesn't
sit next to the deposit in the same block. Add decoys so the set of candidate
deposits is larger than one. Each of these costs something real: latency,
gas, or complexity, and users feel all three.

What makes it interesting rather than depressing is that the requirement isn't
perfect anonymity. It's a large enough set, held for long enough, that the
graph stops being evidence. That's a design parameter you can actually reason
about, argue over, and tune.
