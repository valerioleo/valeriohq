Reading a token standard and implementing it are different activities. These
are the notes I wish I'd had when I started with ERC-7984, the confidential
token standard.

It gets the core decision right: balances are handles to ciphertexts, and the
contract never sees a plaintext amount. That sounds obvious once written down,
but it rules out a whole family of designs where a privileged party can peek
"just for compliance", and being closed off from that early saved me from
building something I'd have had to tear out.

The two things I kept getting wrong:

Failure has to be silent, or you leak. If a transfer reverts when the balance
is too low, the revert tells an observer that the balance was below the
amount. That's an inequality about a number you spent real effort encrypting.
The fix is a transfer that always succeeds structurally and moves zero when it
can't move the requested value, which is deeply strange the first time you
write it.

Allowances are worse than balances. Everything true about a balance is true
about an allowance, plus the approval pattern gives an attacker a way to probe
by watching which spends succeed. I don't think this is fully solved.

Neither of these is a flaw in the standard so much as a place where the old
ERC-20 muscle memory actively works against you.
